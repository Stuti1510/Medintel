# Acute Myocardial Infarction (AMI)

## Overview & Pathophysiology
Acute myocardial infarction occurs due to an acute rupture or erosion of an unstable atherosclerotic plaque in a coronary artery, leading to occlusive or subocclusive thrombus formation and ischemic necrosis of myocardial tissue.

## Key Clinical Findings
- **Hallmark Symptoms**: Crushing, substernal chest pressure, tightness, or squeezing lasting >20 minutes.
- **Radiation**: Left arm, neck, jaw, epigastrium, or interscapular region.
- **Associated Features**: Diaphoresis, dyspnea, nausea, lightheadedness, and impending sense of doom.
- **Atypical Presentations**: Frequently observed in elderly, female, and diabetic patients (isolated dyspnea, nausea, or profound fatigue without overt chest pain).

## Diagnostic Workup
1. **12-Lead ECG**: Emergent within 10 minutes of presentation (evaluates ST-elevation, T-wave inversion, or new LBBB).
2. **Cardiac Biomarkers**: High-sensitivity cardiac troponin I or T (serial measurements at 0, 1, and 3 hours).
3. **Echocardiography**: Identifies regional wall motion abnormalities.

## Clinical Guidance & Discussion Points
- Immediate triage to emergency cardiac facility for primary percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI) or thrombolytic therapy if PCI is unavailable within 120 minutes.
- Hemodynamic stabilization, continuous cardiac rhythm monitoring, and supplemental oxygen if SpO2 <90%.
- Medical therapy discussions with physician: antiplatelet agents (aspirin, P2Y12 inhibitors), anticoagulation, high-intensity statins, and ACE inhibitors/beta-blockers.
