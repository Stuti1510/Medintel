# Basal Cell Carcinoma (BCC)

## Definition
Basal cell carcinoma (BCC) is the most common form of skin cancer worldwide, arising from non-keratinizing cells that originate in the basal layer of the epidermal epidermis and outer root sheath of hair follicles. It rarely metastasizes but causes significant local tissue invasion and destruction if untreated.

## Clinical Appearance
- **Nodular BCC:** Pearly, translucent papule or nodule with prominent overlying arborizing telangiectasia (fine blood vessels) and rolled borders. Often exhibits central depression, crusting, or ulceration ("rodent ulcer").
- **Superficial BCC:** Erythematous, scaly patch or thin plaque, often with a subtle thread-like raised pearly border, commonly located on the trunk and limbs.
- **Morpheaform/Infiltrative BCC:** Indurated, scar-like white-yellow sclerotic plaque with indistinct margins, often more aggressive locally.
- **Pigmented BCC:** Contains brown or black pigment from melanocytes within the tumor, sometimes mimicking melanoma.

## Typical Symptoms & Presentation
- Slow-growing, painless lesion that may bleed easily with minor trauma or gentle wiping.
- Patient often describes a "pimple or sore that won't heal" or bleeds, scabs over, and then recurs.
- Predilection for chronically sun-exposed anatomical regions: face, nose, ears, neck, scalp, and upper trunk.
- Most commonly observed in individuals aged >50 years, with light Fitzpatrick skin phototypes (I-II), and a history of intense intermittent or cumulative ultraviolet (UV) radiation exposure.

## Red Flags
- Rapid enlargement, deep ulceration, or spontaneous persistent bleeding.
- Location in critical cosmetic/functional "H-zone" of the face (eyelids, nose, lips, ears, periauricular region) indicating increased risk of subclinical perineural or deep tissue infiltration.
- Morpheaform/infiltrative appearance with vague borders requiring Mohs micrographic surgery.
- Perineural invasion symptoms: localized paresthesia, tingling, numbness, or motor nerve deficit.

## When to See a Dermatologist
- Any persistent or non-healing skin bump, ulcer, or bleeding spot persisting longer than 4 weeks.
- Prompt clinical evaluation and diagnostic dermoscopy/punch/shave biopsy are indicated for definitive histological confirmation.
