# Acute Ischemic Stroke (AIS)

## Overview & Pathophysiology
Ischemic stroke accounts for approximately 85% of cerebrovascular accidents, resulting from thromboembolic or atherothrombotic occlusion of cerebral arteries leading to focal neural infarction.

## Key Clinical Findings
- **FAST Screening**: Facial droop, Arm drift/weakness, Slurred speech/aphasia, Time to call emergency.
- **Focal Deficits**: Hemiparesis, hemisensory loss, conjugate gaze deviation, homonymous hemianopia, ataxia, or acute confusion.
- **Time of Onset**: Establishing the "last known well" time is paramount for time-sensitive thrombolytic and endovascular eligibility.

## Diagnostic Workup
1. **Non-Contrast Head CT**: Rapidly excludes intracerebral hemorrhage and identifies early ischemic changes (e.g., hyperdense MCA sign).
2. **CT Angiography / Perfusion or Brain MRI (DWI)**: Detects large vessel occlusion (LVO) and salvageable ischemic penumbra.
3. **Point-of-Care Blood Glucose**: Rules out hypoglycemia mimicking acute stroke.

## Critical Emergency Guidance
- Emergent IV thrombolysis (alteplase / tenecteplase) within 4.5 hours of symptom onset if contraindications are absent.
- Mechanical thrombectomy within 6–24 hours for confirmed large vessel occlusion in the anterior circulation.
- Strict blood pressure parameters and immediate admission to a specialized stroke intensive care unit.
